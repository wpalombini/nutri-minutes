Every study we summarise gets an evidence grade. The grade tells you how much the result can be trusted: how likely it is that better research would give a different answer. It is about the study, not about how important the topic is.

## The four levels

Our grades follow the four levels of GRADE, a method widely used in health research to rate evidence. GRADE was designed to rate a whole body of evidence. We use its levels and starting points to rate one study at a time, and our own five factors, below, to move a grade up or down.

- **High certainty:** the true effect is very likely close to this result.
- **Moderate certainty:** the true effect is probably close to this result, but it could be substantially different.
- **Low certainty:** the true effect may be substantially different from this result.
- **Very low certainty:** the true effect is likely to be substantially different from this result.

Each grade is shown in words and as four squares, with one to four filled.

## Where a study starts

The type of study sets the starting point:

- **Randomised trials** start at high certainty, because chance decides who gets the treatment. They move down for problems.
- **Non-randomised trials** start at low certainty. People get a treatment, but chance doesn't decide who, so the groups may differ in other ways.
- **Observational studies** start at low certainty. They follow what people already do, so they can show a link but usually can't show cause and effect. This includes studies that use people's genes to estimate the effect of a diet or habit (Mendelian randomisation).
- **Reviews of studies** start from the certainty of the studies they combine: high for reviews of randomised trials, low for reviews of observational studies or a mix of both, and very low for reviews of animal or lab studies. When a review rates the certainty of its own evidence, we use its rating, and only funding concerns can lower it.
- **Animal and lab studies** start at very low certainty in our grading, because they don't test people.

Every summary shows what kind of study it was, so you know what kind of evidence you are reading.

## What we don't grade

Some papers have no measured results to grade, so we don't grade or summarise them: narrative reviews that don't combine studies in a set way, reports of one or a few patients, study plans, studies based only on interviews, and other papers with no results of their own, such as guidelines.

## What moves a grade

From the starting point, a study's grade can move up or down. A grade moves up one level at most, and never for animal and lab studies, reviews of them, or a review that rated its own evidence. We look at:

- **Design:** how well the study was set up and run, and whether its comparisons were fair. A study that measures people only once, at a single point in time, always loses at least one level for its design, because it can't show which came first.
- **Size:** how many people took part. Small studies give less precise results.
- **Duration:** whether people were followed long enough to see the outcome that matters.
- **Population:** whether the people studied are like the people the result is meant for.
- **Funding and conflicts:** who paid for the study and whether the researchers declared interests that could affect it.

Who pays for a study matters, but it doesn't make a study wrong on its own. We lower a grade for funding only when it comes with a concrete risk: for example, when the company that sells the product tested also ran the analysis, or when a trial changed its main outcome after it started. When a study is funded by the maker of the product tested, we say so next to the grade.

On each study page, "Why this grade" lists what raised and what lowered certainty for that study.

## What a grade doesn't tell you

- A high grade doesn't mean an effect is large or that it applies to you.
- A low grade doesn't mean the result is wrong. It means we can't be sure yet.
- A grade covers one study. Other studies on the same question may point the same way or the other way.
